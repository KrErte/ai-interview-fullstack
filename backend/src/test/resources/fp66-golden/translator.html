Will AI replace Translator?

Not entirely. Today's AI tools can take on about 70% of the typical work in this occupation, mostly: first-draft translation The parts that stay human include: Legal and medical translation where you carry liability; Creative translation and cultural nuance; Quality in smaller languages (such as Estonian).

People who learn to use AI for the automatable parts keep the edge. The 3-phase plan below shows where to start.

AI questions you may get in an interview

More employers now ask how you work with AI. These are typical questions, based on public AI fluency hiring rubrics, not the questions of one employer.

1. How do you use AI in your work today?

What they look for: That you use AI on purpose, with real examples, not just out of curiosity.

A strong answer: Name a specific tool (for example DeepL), one real task such as “First-draft translation”, roughly how much time it saves, and what you still check yourself.

2. Walk us through how you would use AI for first-draft translation.

What they look for: Your actual workflow: what you give the tool, how you check the result and when you redo it.

A strong answer: Describe the steps: the context you provide, the first draft from the AI, how you verify it and what you change. Give one example with the time it took before and after.

3. Tell us about a time AI gave you a wrong or poor result. What did you do?

What they look for: Judgment and accountability: you own the result, not the tool.

A strong answer: A concrete case, for example fluent machine output with a wrong term or a shifted meaning, caught in post-editing against the termbase. Say how you noticed it and what you now do differently.

4. Where would you not use AI in this job, and why?

What they look for: That you know the limits and where people matter most.

A strong answer: Name the part where human judgment counts most, such as “Working with clients”, and explain what could go wrong if a tool did it.

5. How do you handle confidential data when you use AI tools?

What they look for: That the company can trust you with its own and its customers' data.

A strong answer: Client documents are often confidential. Use engines with clear data guarantees, as agreed with the client. Say that you use the tools your employer has approved and remove or anonymise personal data before you share anything.

6. How has the way you use AI changed in the last six months?

What they look for: That you keep learning: AI fluency rubrics look at how your use has developed.

A strong answer: Show a progression: from one-off prompts to a repeatable workflow, a template or an automation, and the result it gave.
