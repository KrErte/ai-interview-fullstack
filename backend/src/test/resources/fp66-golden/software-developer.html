Will AI replace Software developer?

Not entirely. Today's AI tools can take on about 55% of the typical work in this occupation, mostly: writing routine code (crud, boilerplate, integrations) The parts that stay human include: Understanding the business problem and building the right thing; Architectural trade-offs and accountability in production; Critical review and security of AI-generated code.

People who learn to use AI for the automatable parts keep the edge. The 3-phase plan below shows where to start.

A 3-phase AI learning plan

Phase 1

Pair programming with AI

  • Use an agentic coding assistant (Claude Code, Cursor) on real tasks – not just autocomplete.
  • Learn to give context: CLAUDE.md / rules files, tests before code, small steps.
  • Let AI write tests and documentation while you review them.

Project

Take an old project of yours and have an AI agent fix 5 bugs or add 1 feature – time it.

Phase 2

LLMs in your own applications

  • LLM APIs (Anthropic / OpenAI): structured output, tool use, streaming.
  • RAG: embeddings, a vector database (pgvector), chunking.
  • Spring AI or LangChain4j in the Java world.

Project

Add an AI feature to an existing Spring Boot app (e.g. answering questions about a document).

Phase 3

Production-ready AI

  • Evals and regression tests for prompts.
  • Cost, latency, rate limits, caching.
  • Security: prompt injection, data leaks, MCP servers.

Project

Build an MCP server for your company's or project's API and connect it to an AI assistant.

Job interviews · Software developer

AI questions you may get in an interview

More employers now ask how you work with AI. These are typical questions, based on public AI fluency hiring rubrics, not the questions of one employer.

1. How do you use AI in your work today?

What they look for: That you use AI on purpose, with real examples, not just out of curiosity.

A strong answer: Name a specific tool (for example Claude Code), one real task such as “Writing routine code (CRUD, boilerplate, integrations)”, roughly how much time it saves, and what you still check yourself.

2. Walk us through how you would use AI for writing routine code (CRUD, boilerplate, integrations).

What they look for: Your actual workflow: what you give the tool, how you check the result and when you redo it.

A strong answer: Describe the steps: the context you provide, the first draft from the AI, how you verify it and what you change. Give one example with the time it took before and after.

3. Tell us about a time AI gave you a wrong or poor result. What did you do?

What they look for: Judgment and accountability: you own the result, not the tool.

A strong answer: A concrete case, for example code that looked fine but missed an edge case or called an API that does not exist, and how your tests or review caught it. Say how you noticed it and what you now do differently.

4. Where would you not use AI in this job, and why?

What they look for: That you know the limits and where people matter most.

A strong answer: Name the part where human judgment counts most, such as “Clarifying requirements and talking to stakeholders”, and explain what could go wrong if a tool did it.

5. How do you handle confidential data when you use AI tools?

What they look for: That the company can trust you with its own and its customers' data.

A strong answer: Source code, API keys and customer data can leak through an AI tool. Never paste secrets or production data. Say that you use the tools your employer has approved and remove or anonymise personal data before you share anything.

6. How has the way you use AI changed in the last six months?

What they look for: That you keep learning: AI fluency rubrics look at how your use has developed.

A strong answer: Show a progression: from one-off prompts to a repeatable workflow, a template or an automation, and the result it gave.
